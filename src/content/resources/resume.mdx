## The Mindset: Turn AI Experience Into Valuable Resume Content
</div>

**Your resume is more than a list of accomplishments. It is a marketing document.** Recruiters spend **about 10 seconds** on a first pass. They aren't fully reading your resume. They're scanning it for one thing: *why should we interview you instead of someone else?*

That means your resume's job is to tell a short, convincing story about the value you bring. A resume doesn't get you the job. **It gets you the interview.** The interview is where you'll have to show your critical thinking and problem-solving skills — and you likely won't be able to use AI assistance there (though some companies, like Meta, are starting to allow AI for technical problem-solving in interviews).

<ResumeTip title="The one-sentence test" variant="info">
If a recruiter can't explain your value in one clear sentence, they can't advocate for you. Aim for something like: **"CS junior who built a RAG app handling 500+ real queries and can explain the retrieval tradeoffs."** That's specific enough for a recruiter to repeat to a hiring manager without doing any translation work themselves. Write your resume so that sentence is impossible to miss.
</ResumeTip>

<div id="structure-for-students">
## Structure that works for students
</div>

Keep it to **one page**. Use this order — it puts your strongest material where the 10-second scan lands:

1. **Header** — name, email, phone, LinkedIn, GitHub. No full mailing address, no headshot.
2. **Summary** *(2–3 sentences)* — who you are, what you build, and the concrete value you bring.
3. **Education** — school, degree, graduation date. GPA if it helps you.
4. **Experience** — internships, research, part-time work, leadership.
5. **Projects** — treat each project like a job: what you built, the tech, the result.
6. **Skills** — a tight list of specific tools and services, grouped sensibly.

Experience usually goes above Projects — that's the default recruiters expect. But if your projects are stronger than your experience so far (true for many underclassmen), flip the two so your best material lands first.

No internship yet? A deployed RAG app with a GitHub link is stronger evidence than most classroom credentials. Every path in this guide is designed to end in a project you can list. Build one, ship it, link it.

<div id="writing-bullets">
## Writing bullets: context first, then numbers
</div>

Two mistakes recruiters call out constantly on tech resumes:

**1. All achievements, no context.** If every line is a win, the reader loses the thread of what you were actually responsible for. Open each role or project with one summary-style bullet (1–2 lines) that frames the scope. *Then* dive into the wins.

**2. Numbers that show you did it — but not how well.** "Managed a team" says you had a role. It doesn't say whether you were good at it. Recruiters are gauging performance, and a bullet that only proves you did the task leaves them guessing how well you did it.

> **Instead of:** Managed a team.
>
> **Try:** Led a team of 15 and improved performance by 20%.

### The bullet formula

> **[Strong verb]** + **[what you built/did]** + **[tech used]** + **[result or scale]**

Strong verbs: *Led, Built, Designed, Deployed, Reduced, Increased, Automated, Shipped, Streamlined, Spearheaded.*

Examples:
- "Designed and deployed a RAG pipeline on AWS Bedrock with Knowledge Bases, achieving sub-2s retrieval latency on a 50k-document corpus"
- "Built a model card and bias evaluation report for a fine-tuned sentiment classifier, following Google's Responsible AI framework"
- "Configured Claude Code agentic sessions with CLAUDE.md and custom hooks to automate multi-file TypeScript refactors"

<ResumeTip title="No metric? Find the scale" variant="default">
Can't measure impact yet? Quantify scope instead: documents processed, users served, files refactored, hours saved per week, number of cloud regions. A real number beats an adjective.
</ResumeTip>

<ResumeTip title="Does it belong on your resume?" variant="info">
Frame every experience so it answers three questions: **What did I do? What changed because of it? What can I do now that I couldn't before?** If it answers all three, it belongs — that includes research, student orgs, and community work, as long as you name the skills involved.
</ResumeTip>

<div id="what-to-add">
## What to add from this guide
</div>

Each path in this guide is designed to leave you with at least one concrete resume line. Here's how to translate what you did into language that reads well to recruiters and hiring managers.

### Certifications & skill badges

Don't just say "completed course." Name the credential and the platform:

- **Google Cloud** → "Earned Google Cloud Generative AI for Developers skill badge (Vertex AI, model evaluation, responsible AI)"
- **Amazon AWS** → "Completed AWS Generative AI for Developers Professional Certificate; built contextual chatbot with Bedrock Knowledge Bases"
- **Microsoft Azure** → "Completed Azure AI Engineer learning path; deployed RAG-backed chat app using Azure AI Search and OpenAI Service"

### Projects

Frame projects around what they do and what they demonstrate — then let the tech list follow:

> Built a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) pipeline using LangChain, OpenAI embeddings, and Pinecone — grounded LLM responses in a private document corpus, reducing hallucination on domain-specific queries.

That single sentence covers: RAG, LangChain, embeddings, vector databases, and practical AI engineering judgment.

### AI tooling experience

Being able to say you use Claude Code, Cursor, or Copilot in a real workflow is worth listing under tools — but only if you can explain *how*:

> Used Claude Code for agentic code review and multi-file refactors; understand context window tradeoffs and progressive prompting strategies.

That's different from "familiar with AI tools" — it signals actual understanding.

<ResumeTip title="Exact names matter" variant="warning">
ATS software matches keywords literally. "Vertex AI," "Bedrock," and "Azure OpenAI Service" get matched; "worked with cloud AI tools" gets filtered. Use the exact credential, service, and tool names from the job description.
</ResumeTip>

1. **Run VMock first** ([vmock.com/jhu](https://www.vmock.com/jhu)). Free, instant scoring. Revise until you clear **75** — that's the recommended minimum, and a solid target for any tech resume. Don't burn time pushing the score higher; VMock handles formatting, verbs, and structure at scale.
2. **Bring the cleared draft to Life Design Lab drop-in hours.** Monday–Thursday, 1–5 PM ET at the Imagine Center during the academic year — no appointment needed. Walking in with a VMock-cleared draft means the human time goes to the judgment calls software can't make: how you frame projects, what to prioritize for a target role, and how to position non-CS coursework or self-taught skills.
3. **Revise, then tailor to each opportunity.** Load the job posting and your resume into VMock or any AI tool and ask for *suggestions, not rewrites* — you keep control of the final version.

A few more Hopkins-specific notes:

- **No internship yet? Research counts.** Research experience substitutes for internship experience on a first- or second-year resume (and it's often easier to get since we are a research institution). Faculty labs and centers like DSAI host undergraduates.

<div id="gamify-applying">
## When applying gets boring, gamify it 🎮
</div>

Resume done? Now comes the grind — and yes, applying to jobs can get boring and tedious. These tricks come straight from an Amazon intern who made it through recruiting season:

1. **Turn it into a points system.** +5 for an application, +10 for a personalized referral request, +20 for a recruiter response, +50 for an interview. Beat last week's score, or cash your points in for a treat on Friday.
2. **Collect rejections instead of offers.** Set a goal like 100 rejections. It sounds backwards, but it forces you to apply enough to find the right opportunity — every "no" is progress toward the yes.
3. **Spin a wheel after every application.** Have small rewards to help motivate yourself: a matcha, a 10-minute walk, one YouTube video, a sweet treat.
4. **Make every application teach you something.** After each one, write down one thing you learned — a new company, an industry trend, a resume improvement. Even without the job, you still win.

<ResumeTip title="Save this for recruiting season" variant="success">
Rejection is a numbers game, and every application is a rep. Pick a system, keep score, and keep going — YOU GOT THIS!
</ResumeTip>

<div id="the-interview">
## The resume gets the interview. You get the job.
</div>

"My resume speaks for itself" is how interviews get cut short. A resume proves you have experience, but the interview is where you demonstrate how you think, how you work with people, and how you influenced outcomes.

So before you apply, prepare **the story behind every bullet**: the situation, what you did, and what changed because of it. The strongest candidates tell the stories behind their titles and years.

In the interview itself:

- **Ask questions.** It's a two-way street — ask about team culture, daily workflows, how they measure success. Be curious.
- **Be honest about your speed.** If they ask how long something took, tell the truth. They're simply calibrating how you work.
- **Connect experience to judgment.** Explain the thinking behind your resume rather than defending it.

<ResumeTip title="The bottom line" variant="default">
Your resume is more than a list of jobs. It is a marketing tool. Tell the story of the value you've created and the results you've delivered, and let it open the door.
</ResumeTip>
